## A. MAJOR findings

### M1. The corrected cohort still selects on an outcome-associated variable: availability of received/accepted assertions (new; not detectable from the JSON files)

- Quoted text: Methods, "We started from 1,824,388 Crossref records with unique DOIs ... no exclusion depended on retraction status or on reference lists"; Table 1, first row "Crossref records, unique DOI (800 journals) 1,824,388 / 3,526"; Methods, "journals with at least 80% dated records and 150-3,000 dated articles in 2023".
- Source: `code/src/collect_crossref.py` stores only records that carry BOTH a `received` and an `accepted` assertion ("Records without BOTH ... are counted but not stored"). Table 1 therefore does not start from Crossref records but from dated records, and "parsable dates" (row 2) removes nothing because the filter was applied upstream. `data/raw/collect_crossref_summary.tsv` shows the dated share is far below the 80% frame criterion in the retraction-heavy Hindawi-legacy journals over 2021-2026: 2040-2309 Journal of Healthcare Engineering 35% (1,121 of 3,185 records), 1748-6718 Comput Math Methods Med 33%, 1687-5273 Comput Intell Neurosci 36%, 1741-4288 EBCAM 23%, 1563-5147 Math Probl Eng 11%, 1687-7268 J Sensors 54%. The 80% criterion was applied to a 40-record sample of 2023 articles only.
- Quantification (title-matching RW "Journal" to the 802 frame titles, papers dated 2021 or later; approximate, my own matching): 7,776 RW retraction records, of which 3,276 (42%) are in the cohort. In the six journals above RW lists 4,682 retractions and the cohort contains about 314 (7%): e.g. JHE 1,070 RW retractions versus 13 in the cohort; CMMM 1,056 versus 8; MPE 345 versus 1. The lost records are 99.4% peer-review-related (kept: 77%). I confirmed on the Crossref API that three of the lost JHE DOIs (10.1155/2021/6656080, 10.1155/2022/8441050, 10.1155/2021/8218043) are ordinary `journal-article` records of ISSN 2040-2309 published 2021-2022 with an empty `assertion` list: they exist, were retracted for paper-mill/peer-review reasons, and were silently never collected. Among dated records the retraction rate in JHE is about 1%; among all its Crossref records it is about 34% (RW count over Crossref count). Date availability is therefore very strongly associated with the outcome. Stratum-level absence is harmless to a Mantel-Haenszel estimate, but partial dating inside a journal-year that depends on retraction (retracted records losing or never receiving dates) is exactly the within-stratum outcome-dependent selection that C3 produced, and the data cannot show whether it also depends on speed.
- Sensitivity I ran (journal-level dated share = dated/total records from the collection summary): X1 OR for R_process is 6.1 in the 702 journals with dated share >= 90% (290 exposed events), 2.0 in the 98 journals below 90% (87 exposed events, 928 of the 2,639 process events), and 3.1 in the 523 journals >= 95% (45 exposed events; any-retraction OR 1.7). The estimate is not stable to this restriction, in either direction. Mitigating point: the first cohort lost 972 retracted articles in three Hindawi journals and the pooled OR moved only from 4.4 to 4.2, so the pooled value is not very sensitive to which Hindawi records survive; but the year and journal patterns were sensitive then, and are unexamined now.
- Fix: (a) change Table 1 to start with all Crossref journal-article records of the 800 frame journals, split by retracted/not and by dated/undated; (b) report, per publisher and per journal with more than 50 process-related retractions, the share of RW retractions of the journal that are in the cohort; (c) state that the dated-assertion requirement removes about 58% of RW retractions of frame journals (my approximate figure; the authors should compute it with ISSN rather than title matching); (d) add the dated-share sensitivity above as a Results row and discuss; (e) correct the sentence "no exclusion depended on retraction status"; (f) the RECORD checklist (item 6.3/12.2) claims "coverage and unmatched counts in Table 1" - Table 1 contains none; either add them or change the claim.

### M2. "1.8 million articles" and the precision implied by the abstract overstate the effective sample, and the data are dominated by one legacy-Hindawi cluster that the manuscript never names

- Quoted text: Title "analysis of 1.8 million articles"; Discussion "In 1.8 million articles of three large publishers"; Methods "5,625 strata".
- Source: the MH estimate and the logistic and Cox models use only strata with at least one event and one non-event: 111 informative journal-years in 71 journals and 66,969 articles (E_results.json joint_fe_logit; E_results5.json) = 3.7% of the cohort, 2,560 events, with 71 clusters. Taylor & Francis (94 journals, 145,421 articles, 0 process-related retractions) contributes nothing to any association estimate, so "three large publishers" is two. 4,668 (not 5,625) strata have >= 100 articles and can carry X1. Thirty percent of process-related events come from one journal (2314-6141, BioMed Research International, a Hindawi-legacy journal; E_results3 A12) and 67% from five; the word "Hindawi" appears only in reference titles. The reader cannot tell that the "average" is mostly a 2021-2022 Hindawi/Wiley and Springer paper-mill episode.
- Fix: report the number of informative strata, journals and articles in the abstract or first Results paragraph; drop "three publishers" from the Discussion; describe the five largest journals and their publisher lineage in a table (ISSN, title, imprint, events, exposed events, OR); reword the title (for example "... a stratified analysis of Crossref and Retraction Watch records").

### M3. Circular ascertainment is not resolved, and the "not publisher-investigated" analysis rests on 4% of the events

- Quoted text: Abstract, "4.0 for retractions not labelled as publisher investigations"; Results, "restricted the outcome to 107 retractions without the label ... These estimates are similar to the primary one but imprecise"; Conclusions, "specificity to such reasons".
- Source: 96% of process-related retractions (2,526 of 2,639 in RW reasons) carry "Investigation by Journal/Publisher". The restricted set is 107 events in X1-defined strata (113 overall), 21 exposed events, CI 1.2-9.5 (E_results6 A15). That restricted set is not independent of publisher screening; it is simply unlabelled (RW labelling practice), and "investigation by third party" (1,054 events, OR 3.8, CI 0.79-5.8) includes the retractions that publishers acted on after sleuths flagged them. Neither analysis gives an article set whose selection could not have used turnaround time. The prior-art report states that the Wiley/Hindawi integrity whitepaper lists suspect turnaround times among its screening signals; the Discussion mentions "publishers that screen by turnaround" but cites nothing (see M7).
- A useful internal control the authors did not use (my computation, RW reasons): 47% of R_other retractions (382 of 815) also carry the publisher-investigation label. The X1 OR for those publisher-investigated, non-process retractions is 1.14 (378 events, 23 exposed), against 4.19 for process-related ones. Ratio of ORs (process / other), journal-cluster bootstrap 2000 draws: 3.94 (95% CI 1.9-7.6). This is the better evidence for "specificity" than the comparison of 4.2 with 1.06 by eye, and it addresses part of the circularity concern (investigation label alone does not generate the association). It does not remove it, because the reason label itself ("peer review", "paper mill") is assigned by the publisher after the investigation.
- Fix: put circularity in the Abstract limitations (one clause); add the ratio of ORs and the publisher-investigated-other control; state in the Abstract that the label-free estimate rests on 21 exposed events; keep "associated with publisher-detected process failures" and remove "specific to the editorial process" from Abstract/Conclusions unless qualified as "specific to retractions with process-related reasons".

### M4. The headline out-of-time AUC 0.78 is dominated by one journal-year; the "out-of-time" label hides a look-ahead parameter; no journal-risk baseline

- Quoted text: Abstract, "An out-of-time score had a stratified AUC of 0.78 (0.65-0.83), 0.66 without the five journals with most events"; Methods, "a cohort-shift variance estimated earlier in this project".
- Source (my recomputation of the stratified AUC with pair weights): 54 outcome-informative journal-years (selected using the outcome, as the Limitations admit); one stratum, 2314-6141 accepted 2022, carries 55.3% of all positive-negative pairs (533 retracted of 1,472 articles, i.e. 36% retracted, AUC 0.846), the second (1572-817X 2023) 16.0%. Journal 2314-6141 alone is 55.5% of the weight. Excluding that one stratum the AUC is 0.70; the unweighted mean over strata is 0.71 (median 0.76); the project's own sensitivity analyses give 0.71 (2023-24), 0.66 (no top-5), 0.69 (true first-online journals). The 0.78 is thus the AUC of a mass-retraction journal-year, not a representative figure, and the CI (0.65-0.83) is a journal-cluster percentile interval of a ratio dominated by one cluster. The 2022 part of the evaluation population is also left-truncated (received >= 2022-01-01: only 158,197 of 263,985 articles accepted in 2022 have a PIT), the stratum above being one of them.
- Look-ahead: `product/turnaround_forecast/defaults.json` states that tau2 (0.0401) was "learned from all earlier quarterly cohorts (received 2022-01-01..2025-01-01 starts) whose targets were complete by 2026-10-20", i.e. from the same cohorts that are scored. R5 showed the numerical effect is small (tau2 = 0 or 0.1 changes AUC in the third decimal); I did not recompute PIT for the E-cohort (not cheap), so that statement carries over from R5 and should be re-verified by the authors. The text must say so. I found no outcome leakage in the PIT: the visible set uses only `A < t0`, `received < t0` and V in the preceding 365 days, none of which uses retraction information; the `V` fallback to the month-level `issued` date (37% of articles) mis-specifies the "visible/online" window for those journals but cannot leak the outcome because `A < t0` is the binding information constraint.
- Missing comparator: no baseline from journal history. A strict "journal's process-retraction rate among earlier articles, counting only retractions dated before the start of the year" baseline has pooled AUC 0.49 (2023) and 0.46 (2024) in my run, against 0.81 and 0.65 for -PIT (and 0.85 for raw -D in both years), because retraction lags (median 17 months) mean earlier retractions are not yet public when the later cohort is scored. That is a result in the authors' favour that an editor will ask for. Note the 2024 pooled AUC of the score (0.65) is far below that of raw D (0.85): the score is not the best within-year discriminator.
- Fix: report the stratified AUC with a leave-one-stratum-out (or equal-weight stratum) variant next to 0.78 and say in the Abstract "0.66-0.78 depending on exclusion of the largest journals"; disclose that tau2 was estimated on the scored cohorts; add the journal-history baseline and raw-D baseline in Table 5; call the evaluation "retrospective, time-ordered" rather than "prospective".

### M5. The effect of the exposure definition: percentile computed on all articles, including the retracted ones, changes the headline OR by 60%

- Quoted text: Methods, "X1 was a time from received to accepted at or below the 5th percentile of the journal x acceptance-year stratum"; Abstract "fastest 5%".
- Source (my recomputation): the primary OR 4.19 uses strata thresholds in which retracted articles help define the 5th percentile. With thresholds computed on non-retracted articles only: R_process OR 6.89 (R_any 5.03). Ties: the flag is "D <= q05" on integer days, 5.38% flagged, 9.8% of flagged articles sit exactly on the threshold; strict "<" gives 4.14, an exact 5% with random tie-break 4.38. This is not leakage that favours the hypothesis (it attenuates), and for the PIT screen using all articles is the realistic choice, but (a) the strata percentile is an ex post, outcome-contaminated reference, (b) the headline depends on that choice by a factor of 1.6, (c) the manuscript labels the exposure "fastest 5%" without the 5.4% caveat in the abstract/Table 3. D = 0 (same-day received and accepted; 10,163 records, 0.56%) is excluded by rule `d_min = 1`; only 2 of them are retracted (0.02%), so they look like deposit artefacts, but the exclusion removes the most extreme "fast" articles by definition and should be reported.
- Fix: report both thresholds (all-article and non-retracted-reference) and the tie sensitivity in one sentence; state the D = 0 exclusion and its count in Table 1.

## B. MODERATE findings

### Mo1. Negative control: "null", "absent", HE4 "Supported" overstate; the CI type was chosen after the result

- Quoted text: Abstract "a negative-control exposure was null (OR 0.88; cluster CI 0.70-1.16)"; Discussion "absent for a negative-control exposure"; Table 6 "HE4 Negative control null ... Supported (cluster-robust)".
- Source: E_results.json A1 R_process N1: OR 0.876, MH (Robins) CI 0.785-0.977 (excludes 1), cluster CI 0.703-1.163. Any retraction: 0.911, MH 0.829-1.000, cluster 0.745-1.132. The protocol (HE4) says "95% CI includes 1 for all outcomes" without naming the interval; "(cluster-robust)" in Table 6 is a choice made once the MH interval failed. In the first cohort N1 was 1.09 (0.92-1.23, protocol E-A2), so the point estimate moved by about 20% (log scale +-0.1) with the cohort correction alone: that is the realistic size of residual bias in this design. It is smaller than the X1 effect (4.2) but comparable with the evidence offered for weaker exposures (weekend acceptance 1.14, MH CI 1.03-1.28 and cluster CI 0.82-1.72; batch >= 5 1.67). Also, weekend receipt shares neither the special-issue structure nor the ascertainment path of X1, so a null on it cannot bound the biases that matter (M3, M1).
- Fix: write "no clear association (OR 0.88; MH CI 0.78-0.98; cluster CI 0.70-1.16)"; give both intervals in Table 3 for N1 and X5; state that residual bias of about 10-20% cannot be excluded; drop "absent".

### Mo2. Year pattern: the 2020 estimate is a sampling-window artefact; Cochran's Q = 81 ignores clustering; "21 months" is wrong

- Quoted text: Abstract "by acceptance year 11.0 (2020), 5.4 (2021), 9.6 (2022), 2.1 (2023) and 0.79 (2024)"; Results "Cochran's Q = 81, 4 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001"; "when every article had at least 21 months of follow-up".
- Source: (i) collection used `from-pub-date:2021-01-01` (collect_crossref.py), so "acceptance year 2020" contains only articles accepted in 2020 but published in 2021 or later: 59% of the 32,803 articles were accepted in December and 25% in November (my count by month). The 2020 stratum is a left-truncated sample of long-lag articles, with 7 exposed events (cluster CI 5.0-82.4; three of the seven from one journal). It should not appear in the abstract. (ii) Q is computed from Robins-Breslow MH variances (A19), which ignore journal clustering; the cluster CIs for the same years are several times as wide on the log scale (2021: MH 4.4-6.5, cluster 2.3-12.9). With a journal-cluster bootstrap (2000 draws) of the year-specific log ORs I get Q = 14.6 (df 4), p = 0.006, bootstrap SEs 0.81, 0.48, 0.43, 0.41, 0.67; a slope over 2021-24 of -0.73 log-OR per year (95% CI -1.20 to -0.15); ratio of pooled 2023-24 OR to pooled 2021-22 OR 0.24 (95% CI 0.08-0.82). So the decline is supported, but the test statistic is overstated five-fold and the series is non-monotone (5.4 then 9.6). (iii) A11 restricts to articles accepted up to 2024-10-31 and retractions within 730 days; with the 2026-11-24 data cut the minimum follow-up is 24.8 months, not 21 (the "21" originates in the protocol text of E-A3 and is hard-coded in the builder).
- Interpretation: the sentences "has weakened for recent articles" and "so the decline is not an artefact of unequal follow-up" are acceptable after the Q correction, with the 12 exposed events in 2024 stated in the Abstract ("compatible with no association or a threefold association"); the calendar-wave confound is correctly disclosed.
- Fix: delete 2020 from the Abstract (or flag truncation); replace Q by the cluster-aware test (or a slope/interaction with cluster bootstrap); correct "21 months".

### Mo3. Journal-level meta-analysis (A16): design choices that move the pooled estimate and the interpretation

- Source (`e_analysis6.py` and my reanalysis): journals are selected by having >= 10 events (outcome-conditioned); the journal log OR is the MH estimate but its standard error is the crude 2x2 formula 1/a+1/b+1/c+1/e of the collapsed table, which understates the MH variance (e.g. 1866-7538: 0.137 versus Robins 0.199); the 0.5 continuity correction is applied (to the crude table, replacing the MH estimate) only when a cell is zero, so journals with one exposed event (four of the 20) enter uncorrected and three with zero exposed events are corrected; the prediction interval uses 1.96 instead of t(k-2) (t-based: 0.29-27.1). Sensitivity: with Robins SEs and the 17 journals where MH is defined, pooled OR 3.22 (1.89-5.51), I2 88%; Hartung-Knapp CI for the authors' set 1.47-5.40; journals with >= 3 exposed events (12 journals) 5.00 (2.82-8.86), I2 93%; crude+0.5 for all 2.90 (1.95-4.33), I2 81%. The conclusion (large heterogeneity, PI covering 1) is robust; the pooled value (2.8-5.0) and "I2 91%" are not.
- Interpretation issue: Discussion, "this suggests that the average relation is dominated by a limited number of episodes in which compromised handling produced fast acceptance in bulk, probably in guest-edited collections". The data contain no special-issue information, the sentence is a hypothesis; "probably" is unsupported. Also leave-one-out holds tau2 fixed.
- Fix: report the sensitivity range, use Robins SE and a common continuity correction, t-based PI, Hartung-Knapp; replace "probably" by "possibly" and mark as hypothesis.

### Mo4. Bootstrap, standard errors and in-text intervals

- Quoted text: Methods "300-500 draws"; Results "acceptance within 7 days had OR 1.09 (Mantel-Haenszel CI 0.78-1.53)"; "Absolute speed was not informative".
- Source: B is 50-500 depending on the analysis (A19 uses 50; Table 4 rows use 200-300). Monte Carlo error is visible: the primary cluster CI is 2.15-7.26 in A1 (B = 500), 1.99-6.95 in A17 (B = 300, plotted as "primary" in Figure 4b), 2.16-7.29 / 2.10-6.92 / 2.07-7.52 over three seeds with B = 500 in my run, 2.07-7.11 with B = 5000. A single `rng` is shared across calls (results depend on call order), percentile intervals with silently dropped non-finite draws are used for a ratio with extreme skew, and the "0.00" lower limits mean that at least 2.5% of draws had zero exposed events (for X3, 80% of flagged events are in one journal, which is absent from 37% of resamples). The joint model uses a journal-cluster sandwich with 71 clusters and no small-sample correction (t-based interval with G-1 df: 2.34-6.33, immaterial here); Holm over two tests whose p-values are 9e-8 and 9e-6 is cosmetic. The in-text sentence on X3 prints the narrow MH interval (0.78-1.53) next to the Table 3 cluster interval 0.00-2.74 and concludes "not informative": an interval running from 0 to 2.7 is uninformative, not evidence of no signal.
- Cox: the code censors R_process analyses at the data cut for articles retracted for other reasons (competing retraction not censored at its date). I re-ran it with those articles censored at retraction: HR 3.153 versus 3.159, so no effect; but the manuscript should state the cause-specific definition. The Cox model adds little beyond the stratified logit (follow-up inside a journal-year is nearly equal) and no proportional-hazards check is shown; bootstrap CI uses 200 resamples.
- PPV and AUC intervals: journal-cluster percentile bootstrap for PPV/lift/sensitivity is the correct family (Wilson/Clopper-Pearson intervals, 61/8,963 gives about 0.52%-0.87%, would ignore that 36% of flagged events are in one journal), but the 36 events-bearing journals and top-journal share (36% of flagged events at 1%, 50% at 5%) must be shown beside it; PPV for 2022-24 is a lower bound because 2024-accepted articles have 23-34 months of follow-up while the median lag to retraction is 17 months (only 0.07% of articles accepted in 2024 are process-retracted, against 0.42% for 2021).
- Fix: B >= 2,000 with the same seed protocol, BCa or studentised intervals for the primary result, state the number of non-finite draws, print cluster CIs everywhere in the text, and describe the PPV as a lower bound.

### Mo6. "Weak screen", "graded" and "robust": wording versus evidence

- "As a screen it is weak, since most flagged articles are not retracted" (Abstract and Conclusions). The reasoning is invalid as stated: with a 0.18% base rate almost any screen has a PPV of a few percent at most; what the data show is enrichment (lift 3.6-3.8, stratified AUC 0.66-0.78, pooled 0.79) with low absolute yield (about 147 flagged articles per retraction), plus no stated comparison with doing nothing or with simple alternatives. Reword: "modest enrichment, low yield"; add that unretracted problematic articles lower the observed PPV.
- "Graded dose-response": Table/Figure show ORs 5.5 (0-5th), 6.1 (5-10th), 4.9, 3.9, 3.2 ... down to 0.07 (90-95th) and 0.16 (95-100th). The fast tail is flat (PPV 0.68% for PIT <= 0.01 versus 0.66% for <= 0.05; retraction rate 6.8 vs 6.5 per 1,000 in the two lowest bands) and the end bin is non-monotone. Further, a monotone gradient over the whole within-stratum rank distribution is what a fast-tail concentration of retracted articles in a few strata produces mechanically for the slower bins, so the slow-side OR 0.10 is not independent confirmation. Reword to "decreasing across the distribution, with a plateau among the fastest 10%".
- "Robust": the range across Table 4 is 2.8 (no top-5 journals) to 6.7 (Springer) or 6.4 (no top-20); removal of mass-retraction journal-years leaves 60 exposed events of 377 and 399 of 2,560 events. Direction is stable; the magnitude is not. "Robust in direction, magnitude 2.8-6.7" is accurate. The analysis excluding the 20 journals with most events (OR 6.4, 39 exposed events, 151 events) argues against the "few journals" explanation of the association and should be said plainly in the Discussion, which currently leans on that explanation.
- "Specific": supported by the ratio of ORs 3.9 (1.9-7.6), not by the printed comparison of 4.2 with 1.06 (HE3 in Table 6 is judged by point estimates although the protocol defines it as a ratio of ORs).

[18] (Zaw et al., Cochrane reviews with multiply retracted authors) and [19] (Studd et al., notice type and citations) do not "map retraction reasons". ## C. MINOR findings

1. "107 retractions without the label" (Results) versus "113 of these did not carry the label" (preceding paragraph): the first is within X1-defined strata. Same for 1,054 third-party (X1 strata) versus 1,124 overall. Say "in strata with X1 defined".

## E. Top five reasons an editor would still reject (novelty, impact, AI use), and what a human author must do

1. Impact: the finding is a description of a historical episode, not a transferable signal. About one-third of the process-related events come from one Hindawi-legacy journal and two-thirds from five; the association is 5.4 -> 9.6 -> 2.1 -> 0.79 over 2021-2024 with 12 exposed events in 2024; the journal-level I2 is 88-93% with a prediction interval including 1; imprint Blackwell is null with 431 events. The paper's own conclusion is that the signal is a weak screen and "must not be used to judge individual papers". RIPR readers ask what an editorial office can do on Monday. Human action: reframe as a retrospective analysis of the 2020-2022 manipulation wave; add the group-level analysis the Discussion recommends but does not test (for example journal-month shares of fast or batch acceptances followed by later retraction waves), because the effect is a group phenomenon.
2. Novelty versus circularity: the headline relation is partly produced by the detection process (96% of process retractions are publisher investigations; Wiley's own screening used turnaround). The non-circular check has 21 exposed events. An editor will read this as "publishers retract what they flagged by speed". Human action: lead with this limitation, add the investigated-other control and ratio of ORs (M3), and obtain from the publisher (or the authors' contacts at Wiley/Springer) the retraction trigger for a sample of notices.
3. Validity of the cohort and of the denominator: retracted articles without date assertions were never collected (M1); the claim of a corrected cohort is only half-true. Human action: rebuild the cohort starting from all journal-article records, report date availability by retraction status and journal, and run the restrictions in M1; consider extracting dates from publisher pages for the lost Hindawi-legacy retractions (a human, not the model, must decide if that is feasible).
4. AI use and independence: the pipeline, drafting, literature search, "adversarial review" and "reproducibility audit" were all done by instances of one model family; the Limitations say there was "no human expert review before submission"; the project was already rejected by RIPR for low novelty and AI use. RIPR's policy requires authors to take responsibility and describe use. Human action: a named human biostatistician must independently re-run e_build.py to e_analysis6.py from the raw files, confirm the headline numbers in the table in section 0, review every sentence of the Discussion, and sign the Authors' contributions; remove the machine-generated phrasing and the sentence about the previous rejection from Methods; state concretely what the human authors did.
5. Reproducibility and housekeeping signal a template product: unarchived branch with an AI-named branch label, raw data committed, placeholders in Declarations and a leftover note about reference abbreviations, hard-coded text strings in the builder (the "21 months" error), four preprint/blog references doing the novelty argument, and a self-described "autonomous" workflow. Human action: Zenodo deposit with a DOI and file hashes, fill declarations, fix reference style, register the final analysis plan externally if a revised study is run on the corrected cohort.

## F. Verdict

Major revision; not suitable for submission in the present form. The arithmetic is accurate and reproducible: every headline number I recomputed from the processed data (the X1 MH ORs of 4.19 and 3.11, the negative control 0.88, the A16 pooled 2.82 with I2 91%, the stratified AUC 0.78, the PPV 0.68%, the joint logit 3.85 and the Cox HR 3.16) matches. The conclusions that survive my checks are modest and appropriately cautious in the final paragraph: within journal-year, the fastest 5% had about four-fold odds of a later process-related retraction (cluster interval about 2.1-7.1 with adequate B), strongly heterogeneous across journals (PI about 0.3-23) and years (cluster-aware Q 14.6, p 0.006; late/early ratio 0.24, 0.08-0.82), with a weaker association for other retraction reasons (ratio of ORs 3.9, 1.9-7.6). What does not survive is the framing: the cohort remains selected on an outcome-associated variable (undated Crossref records, concentrated in retracted Hindawi-legacy papers; about 58% of RW retractions of frame journals are not in the cohort by my approximate count), the effective sample is 111 journal-years in 71 journals with one journal carrying 30% of events, the out-of-time AUC headline is dominated by one journal-year (55% of pairs; 0.70 without it) and uses a bandwidth learned on the scored cohorts, the negative control is described as null although the MH interval excludes 1, the Q statistic is overstated five-fold, the 2020 estimate is a sampling-window artefact, a pre-specified imprint analysis with a null in a 431-event imprint is omitted, and circular ascertainment is acknowledged but not bounded. With the analyses in M1-M5, the interval and wording corrections in Mo1-Mo8, and the human work in section E, the paper could be a useful, honest RIPR research article reporting a heterogeneous, time-limited association of modest screening value; as it stands, an editor would reasonably reject it on impact and on the extent of undisclosed-by-human verification.
