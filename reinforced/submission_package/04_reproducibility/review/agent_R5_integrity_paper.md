## A. MAJOR findings

### M1. Cleaning rule C3 differentially deletes 28% of retracted articles, almost all fast, process-related Hindawi papers (selection on the outcome)
- Quoted text: Methods/Data, "cleaning rules: duplicate DOIs, durations below 1 or above 1,460 days, acceptance more than seven days after first online publication, non-research items by title or fewer than five references". Results, "Of 1,737,272 articles, 2,399 (0.14%) were retracted and 1,664 (0.10%) for peer-review-related reasons".
- Source/recomputation: Rebuilding the cohort from `data/raw/crossref` with the project's own `tdelay.data.clean` shows that 3,526 retracted DOIs (RetractionNature = Retraction) exist in the 800 frame journals, but only 2,399 survive. Rule C3 removes 972 retracted articles (27.6%) but only 0.69% of non-retracted ones. The cause is an artefact: in the Hindawi journals 2314-6141 (730 articles), 1687-5273 (173) and 1687-7268 (64), Crossref has no `first_online`, so `V` is silently replaced by `issued`, which is a placeholder such as 2021-01-15. That date precedes acceptance by 10-336 days (mean 176), so the article is judged "accepted after publication" and dropped. In the dropped set 99.4% are process-related, median D is 44.5 days, and 37.6% are in the fastest 5% of their journal-year (kept process-related retractions: 16.8%). Among the 1,664 retained process events only 69 come from 2314-6141, whereas the full data hold 793 (the largest journal). `data.clean` also silently uses `V = first_online.fillna(issued)` for 36% of all articles, with missing day set to the 15th (19% of those).
- Effect (my rebuild, C3 applied only where a real `first_online` exists, all else unchanged): process events 2,553 in X1-defined strata (vs 1,590); pooled MH OR 4.26 (cluster 2.15-6.91; any retraction 3.12) - the pooled number is stable, but the published year pattern is not. By acceptance year: 2021 OR 5.4 (manuscript 10.0), 2022 OR 9.6 (manuscript 6.3), 2023 2.1, 2024 0.81. Wiley OR 2.85 (manuscript 2.1). Top-5 journals hold 69% (manuscript 59%), top 20 hold 95% (90%). The abstract headline "OR 10.0 in 2021 and 6.3 in 2022" is partly a product of the exclusion, and the year narrative of a 2020-2022 peak followed by a decline is not what the full data say. Removing the further refs < 5 and title rules changes pooled OR only to 4.17-4.19.
- Fix: replace C3 by "only if a genuine first-online date exists" (or drop C3 for the main analysis), rerun everything (including A3, A4, A11, A12 and the PIT visible set), and add a cohort-flow figure/table with counts removed per rule split by retracted/non-retracted. At minimum report this as a sensitivity analysis and rewrite the abstract and Discussion on the corrected year pattern. Describe `V` accurately in Methods.

### M2. Differential outcome ascertainment that is partly caused by the exposure is not discussed (circularity)
- Quoted text: Limitations, "Outcome: retraction is a late, incomplete and publisher-dependent marker; unretracted problematic articles are counted as controls, which would bias the odds ratio towards one". Results, "The association was specific to retractions involving the editorial process".
- Source: 1,557 of the 1,664 process-related retractions also carry the Retraction Watch reason "Investigation by Journal/Publisher". The prior-art report (section 1) notes that the Wiley/Hindawi whitepaper lists suspect turnaround times among its screening signals, and Bishop 2023 and Richardson 2025 used short turnaround to flag editors/special issues. Mass retractions are therefore identified by investigations in which timing anomalies are one selection criterion. This produces a positive exposure-outcome association by construction (detection bias), independent of any link to actual review failure. It also predicts exactly the pattern read as "specificity" (OR 0.83 for other reasons, which arise through other routes) and the whole-distribution gradient. The negative control N1 (weekend receipt) is not used by any investigator and so cannot detect this bias. The manuscript asserts only bias toward the null.
- Fix: state this as the principal threat to validity; temper "specific to the editorial process" and "graded" to "consistent with"; add an analysis restricted to retractions whose reason is not "Investigation by Journal/Publisher" (or to notices published before publishers' timing-screening was announced), and an exposure-independent negative-control outcome. Do not present the OR as evidence about review quality.

### M3. The conclusion "journal-relative, not absolute, speed should be the basis" is contradicted by the paper's own screening results
- Quoted text: Discussion, "absolute speed (within 7 days) ... carry no signal once journal is accounted for suggests that tools based on fixed thresholds will flag many fast-lane articles without benefit"; Conclusions, "Journal-relative timing, not absolute speed, should be the basis for any editorial-office monitoring".
- Source: Table 3: "Accepted within 7 days" has the highest PPV (0.51%) and lift (4.5) of all flags, higher than the score in the lowest 1% (0.46%, 4.0). E_results2 A9: pooled AUC of -D is 0.84 (0.69-0.91) versus 0.77 for -PIT and 0.66 for batch. The within-stratum MH OR for X3 is 1.14, but its cluster interval is 0.00-3.00 (E_results.json A1 R_process X3; the manuscript prints the narrower MH interval 0.82-1.60, see M4). A within-journal null with an interval spanning 0 to 3 is not evidence of "no signal", and the pooled evidence shows absolute speed discriminates best across journals. The sentence "probably because the journals with mass retractions also differ in absolute speed" is speculation, not tested; article type is not observed so "fast-lane" is likewise untested.
- Fix: report the X3 cluster CI, remove the recommendation, or support it with a screening comparison that adjusts for journal-level priors; present the pooled-versus-stratified AUC contrast as the main screening finding.

### M4. Inconsistent and selectively narrower confidence intervals in Table 2, and small-effective-cluster inference
- Quoted text: Table 2 caption, "* Mantel-Haenszel interval, shown where the bootstrap interval was unstable because of few events"; "Slowest 5% ... 0.13 (0.07-0.23)*"; "Accepted within 7 days 1.14 (0.82-1.60)*".
- Source: E_results.json A1 R_process X2 ci_cluster = [0.036, 0.298] (finite and stable), ci_mh = [0.074, 0.228]; X3 ci_cluster = [0.0, 3.0], ci_mh = [0.82, 1.60]. In both starred rows the printed interval is the narrower one, and it ignores clustering, which is the main source of uncertainty (X1: MH 3.8-5.2 vs cluster 2.1-8.2). The bootstrap used 500 draws (A1) but only 200-300 for A2, A4, A8, A11 and A13, while Methods says "500 draws"; the 2.5% quantile of 200 draws rests on 5 draws; percentile intervals are used for a statistic with extreme skew and NaN draws (denominator zero) silently discarded (`bs[np.isfinite(bs)]`).
- Joint model: the headline "adjusted 3.6 (2.4-5.6), Holm-adjusted p < 0.001" comes from a journal-clustered sandwich with 69 clusters (106 informative strata) of which five journals carry 59% of events, with no small-sample correction (p = 3e-9 overstates evidence). The unconditional fixed-effects logit with stratum dummies is also affected by the incidental-parameters problem for strata with few events, and a 1e-3 ridge was added to the dummies (E-A1).
- Fix: show cluster bootstrap CIs for all rows (state the lower bound 0.0 honestly), use at least 2,000 resamples (BCa or studentised), consider wild-cluster bootstrap or conditional logistic regression, and report the effective numbers of informative journals and journal-years (69 and 106 for R_process).

### M5. Residual confounding by calendar time and special issues; ORs fall with finer strata
- Quoted text: "which removes differences between journals and years and equalises follow-up".
- Source (my recomputation, same exposure X1, same data): stratifying by journal x acceptance quarter gives OR 3.79 (any retraction 2.63), journal x month gives 3.34 (any 2.36), versus 4.45 / 2.97 with journal-year. Mass-retraction waves are tied to dated special issues, so within-year acceptance date is a confounder; it is also correlated with publication date and therefore follow-up. The manuscript offers no analysis by special-issue/article type (a Crossref `type`/collection field or title regex is available), and "equalises follow-up" is true only at acceptance-year granularity.
- Fix: add journal x month (or journal x publication-month) MH and fixed-effects results as a main sensitivity; add a special-issue/article-type adjustment where derivable; give an E-value.

### M6. Year decline is over-read, partly artefactual (see M1), and the 2024 "null" is a low-power estimate
- Quoted text: Abstract, "null in 2024 (0.81)"; Results, "This is not an artefact of unequal follow-up"; Discussion, "which survives equal follow-up, suggests either that the manipulation ... has become less common ...".
- Source: 2024 MH OR 0.81, cluster CI 0.29-3.82, 12 exposed events (E_results.json A4 by_year); 2023 cluster CI 0.98-4.21. The 24-month window (A11) equalises the clock since acceptance, not the calendar: retractions occur in publisher-driven waves, so a 2021 cohort's window ends in 2023 (peak sweep) and a 2024 cohort's in late 2026. No test of trend or of year interaction is given, and the 2020 estimate rests on 7 exposed events (cluster CI 4.5-113). With M1 the 2021 and 2022 values change.
- Fix: test interaction (exposure x year) after correcting M1, say "interval compatible with no association or with a threefold increase" for 2024, and describe the A11 limitation. Limit the interpretive sentence to the data actually supporting it.

### M7. Out-of-time evaluation claims exceed what is shown
(a) Look-ahead. `tau2 = 0.0401` comes from `product/turnaround_forecast/defaults.json`, learned (note in that file) "from all earlier quarterly cohorts received 2022-01-01..2025-01-01 ... targets complete by 2026-10-20", i.e. the same cohorts being scored. The manuscript says only "learned earlier in this project" and in the abstract/discussion treats the score as computed "only from a journal's earlier articles". My sensitivity (PIT recomputed with tau2 = 0 and 0.10): stratified AUC 0.707 / 0.705 (reported 0.706), lift at lowest 1% 4.1 / 4.7 (reported 4.0). Immaterial numerically, but the claim must be corrected. Additionally the journal frame (>= 80% dated records, 150-3,000 dated articles in 2023) conditions on later data for 2022 origins.
(b) A3 population is truncated. Requiring PIT availability means receipt >= 2022-01-01, so only 59% of 2022-accepted articles are included (median D 96 days vs 182 for those excluded; 2023: 96% included; 2024: 99%). The population is therefore not "accepted 2022-2024" but "received and accepted 2022-2024", short-duration-biased in 2022 (7.1% of 2022 articles have PIT <= 0.05 vs 4.5% in 2024). The 2021 acceptance peak (678 process events) is outside A3 entirely; events in A3 are 114 / 495 / 215 for 2022 / 2023 / 2024. Also the origin grid (H = 91 days with quarterly origins) leaves received dates on day 92 of Q3 and Q4 unscored and overlaps one day in Q1, a negligible but undocumented detail.
(c) Stratified AUC is conditioned on outcomes. `strat_auc` keeps only journal-years that contain both events and non-events: 54 journal-years from 36 journals, determined using the outcome, unknowable ex ante. Pair-weights are very uneven: one stratum (1572-817X_2023) carries 36% of pairs and the top five 69%. The AUC is within-stratum discrimination, not a screening AUC; the screening-relevant pooled AUC is a different number (0.77 / 0.84).
(d) Table 3 gives no uncertainty. The flagged-1% events are concentrated: 22 of the 40 retracted articles come from one journal (1868-7873) and 9 from another (1742-481X). Excluding the three journals with most events lifts the lowest-1% lift to 8.3. PPV and lift need journal-cluster CIs. The outcome is right-censored for 2024-accepted articles (22-34 months follow-up), so PPV is a lower bound that varies by year.
(e) "Prospective screening" in A3's name is a retrospective evaluation with fixed labels. Add a rolling-origin version (train/benchmark on year t, evaluate on t+1) and baselines (journal's prior-year retraction count; raw D; combination), as the prior-art report shows prior-retraction-rate beats citation-based predictors in the nearest design (Arjomandi 2026).

### M8. Causal and mechanism-laden language
- "specific to the editorial process", "graded relation", "specificity to such reasons and robustness to mass-retraction events" (Conclusions); "the manipulation that produced fast acceptances in 2020-2022 has become less common" (Discussion). Observed data show only association within journal-year strata with outcome ascertainment possibly depending on the exposure (M2). "Robustness to mass-retraction events" is partly false: the batch exposure is not robust; for X1, excluding the 16 mass strata removes 76% of process events (1,590 -> 383), yet the paper presents persistence of OR 3.4 as robustness without a heterogeneity analysis. The "concentration" framing in the Abstract/Conclusions ("mainly in a small number of journals") contradicts the data: removing the top 20 journals raises the OR from 4.4 to 6.6 (A12), i.e. the events are concentrated but the association is not confined to those journals. Fix: align Abstract/Conclusions with A12.

## B. MODERATE findings

### Mo1. "Monotone dose-response" is not monotone
- Quoted: Abstract "with a monotone dose-response". Source (A2): the fastest bin (0-5th percentile) OR 6.18 is below the 5-10th bin OR 7.59; 90-95th 0.148 vs 95-100th 0.225; non-monotone around bins 13-15 (0.37, 0.42, 0.47). Cluster CIs overlap broadly. Fix: "graded overall with a plateau at the fast end"; the plateau/peak at 5-10th percentile is itself interesting (the extreme tail is not the riskiest).

### Mo2. Ties in percentile flags (X1 uses D <= q05 on integer days)
- Source (my recomputation): realized X1 prevalence is 5.35% rather than 5% (per-stratum 5.0%-18.1%; 32 strata exceed 8%, 1 exceeds 15%); 7,944 flagged articles (8.7% of the exposed) sit exactly on the threshold. Sensitivity: strict "<" gives OR 4.11 (n exposed 83,157); exact 5% with random tie-break gives 4.44-4.50 (any retraction 2.94-2.98); average-rank percentile <= 0.05 gives 4.80. Tie handling is therefore a minor issue (range 4.1-4.8), but it should be stated that the exposure is "at or below the 5th percentile (5.4% flagged)", and the sensitivity shown. X2 (>= q95) has the same tie issue; the X2 ORs (0.13, 12 events) are the most sensitive.

### Mo3. Outcome-contaminated covariates and cleaning
- Retracted Wiley records often have stripped references (`n_refs = 0` for 42 retracted articles; median refs 30 vs 43), so ln_refs in the joint model is post-outcome metadata, and the n_refs < 5 rule removed 84 retracted articles (2.4% vs 2.9% of others; small). ln_refs/ln_auth were imputed with the median for missing values. Retracted titles carry "RETRACTED:" (99.2% contain "retract") in the Crossref snapshot, so the "non-research by title" rule only avoided removing them by accident (pattern "retraction" does not match "retracted"). Fix: drop ln_refs as a covariate (or take references from an archived snapshot) and document.

### Mo4. "Computer-generated content OR 9.0" is mostly the same events as R_process
- 438 of 522 R_ai records (84%) are also R_process; AI-only (84 articles) gives OR 0.44 (2 exposed). The sentence "For computer-generated content the odds ratio was 9.0" and the Table 1 column suggest an independent third outcome. Report the overlap and the AI-only estimate; "not driven by computer-generated content (OR 3.0 without it)" is correct (my 2.96).

### Mo5. Negative-control design is weak
- Weekend receipt shares neither the confounding structure (special issues, publisher sweeps, journal-year) nor the ascertainment path with X1, so a null is uninformative about the main biases (M2, M5). A more useful control: slow-acceptance exposures (already OR 0.13, which shows a monotone gradient but not specificity), placebo outcomes, or a pseudo-exposure such as the percentile of the lag/other process times within the same article. Also HE4 was specified "for all outcomes" but only R_process and R_any are reported; the external N1 (R_process OR 0.49) is not mentioned.

### Mo6. Publisher/imprint heterogeneity under-reported
- A4 by_imprint (pre-specified) shows Blackwell OR 0.92 (cluster 0.0-1.73; 24 exposed events, 431 events), Wiley 4.8, Springer 6.8, BMC 7.8, other 10.1. The manuscript reports only publisher-level (Springer Nature 6.8; Wiley 2.1). The null in an imprint with 431 retractions undermines the pooled claim and should be shown.

### Mo7. External replication reported selectively
- Quoted: "the replication is uninformative rather than contradictory". Source (E_results2 A7): R_process X1 OR 1.34 (0.0-3.89), but R_any X1 OR 0.62 (0.0-1.61, 5 exposed events), and batch >= 10 gives OR 4.7 (cluster 1.26-13.4), which supports HE2 in the external data and is not mentioned. Also "118 journals" (Methods) vs "122 journals" (Results and JSON). Describe all external estimates or none.

### Mo8. Time-to-retraction comparison is confounded
- "median 12 versus 16 months, p < 0.001": fast-flagged retracted articles are concentrated in the 2021-2022 cohort (retraction wave), so the difference mostly reflects cohort; the Mann-Whitney test ignores acceptance year and clustering (299 vs 2,000 articles from few journals). `rdate` takes the first RW record (`groupby.first`), which may not be the earliest notice. Present stratified by year or drop.

### Mo9. Accepted-to-online lag measurement is poor
- 36% of articles have no `first_online`; `V` is the `issued` date (month-precision, day defaulted to the 15th for 19%). L1 (5th percentile, median lag 20 days) is therefore strongly mismeasured, and "lag was unrelated to retraction" (L1 MH 0.72, 0.57-0.91 not null on the MH interval) is uninformative. Restrict A13 to real `first_online` dates.

### Mo10. Batch exposure not size-normalised
- X4 (>= 5 same-day acceptances) is 39% of articles and depends on journal volume; within-stratum MH adjusts level, but the exposure is nearly constant in large journals. Define batch relative to the journal's expected daily rate (e.g., above 99th percentile of the journal's daily count).

### Mo11. Pre-specification description
- Methods says the amendments added "a negative-control and subtype analysis", but N1/HE4 was in the original protocol (E1). The git history supports the claimed ordering (protocol commit c2139be 19:40, data builder 19:43, script 19:43, first results 19:45, later amendments committed before their scripts), but all within 25 minutes and with the same agent specifying the amendments after seeing the results (E-A2 quotes first results); the pre-specification adds little weight for the amended analyses, and the manuscript should say HE1-HE5 and A1-A6 are the only pre-first-look analyses. Also the repository now contains E-A5 (Cox, A14, `E_results5.json`) committed after the manuscript: the paper says "E-A1 to E-A4", so either update or confirm the manuscript version.

### Mo12. Unsupported or over-generalised statements and novelty wording
- "Machine-learning models of retraction have not used timing features [14]": one study; prior-art lists three. "consistent with how publishers describe their own integrity screening" has no citation (whitepaper not read). The statement that ecological association "has the opposite sign to the within-journal one" compares a different exposure (share <= 7 days, rho -0.07) with X1; for batch, rho is +0.19. The novelty claims are properly hedged ("we found no earlier study ... our searches did not cover Scopus, Web of Science"), consistent with the prior-art report; however the report's recommended design precedent (Arjomandi 2026, frozen-T0 journal-by-year strata) and overlapping lag-prediction plan (Dianovics 2025) are not cited, and Harvey 2026 and Toraih 2025 are unread/unmentioned. Add them.

## D. Assessment against the format of a research article in RIPR

Structure (Background, Methods, Results, Discussion, Limitations, Conclusions, Declarations) is correct and the length is appropriate. Reporting is below the standard of a RECORD/STROBE-conformant observational study: there is no participant/article flow diagram, no baseline table by exposure status (journal size, year, publisher, article type, covariates), no supplement of all analyses with n events per cell, no assessment of data completeness of the date fields by publisher and year (the prior-art report flags that missingness correlates with journal type), and no cohort-derivation counts. The statistics are respectable in intent but the inference (few effective clusters, sparse events, post hoc strata) is under-described. The AI-use statement is candid. The paper is publishable in scope but needs the corrections above before it reaches the level of a typical RIPR research article.

### Three changes that would most raise quality
1. Fix and document the cohort (M1): reconstruct the sample without the C3 artefact, report a flow diagram with counts removed per rule by retraction status, rerun all analyses, and rewrite the year pattern and concentration figures. This is the largest correctness issue.
2. Confront outcome-ascertainment circularity and residual confounding (M2, M5): analyses restricted to non-investigation-triggered retractions, journal x month strata and special-issue/article-type adjustment, a journal-level forest plot / random-effects summary with an interaction test for year, and honest cluster-based inference (wild-cluster bootstrap, effective cluster counts). Reframe causal wording accordingly.
3. Rebuild the screening evaluation (M3, M7): a true rolling-origin out-of-time design including all acceptance years, journal-cluster CIs for PPV/lift/NNS, baselines (journal's prior retraction count; absolute D; combined), a PR curve, and a consistent, evidence-based recommendation (the current "relative not absolute" advice is contradicted by Table 3 and the pooled AUC).

## Verdict

Major revision (not acceptable in current form). The data link is genuine and the headline association (fast acceptance within journal-year, OR about 4 for process-related retraction, near 0.13 for slow acceptance) is numerically reproducible and survives tie handling, tau2 changes and removal of the largest journals. However, (i) the cohort construction silently removes 28% of retracted articles, disproportionately the fast Hindawi process-related ones, because of a first-online placeholder, which alters the year pattern and journal concentration emphasised in the abstract; (ii) the exposure is plausibly part of how publishers selected papers for investigation and retraction, which the manuscript does not acknowledge and which the negative control cannot address; (iii) the confidence intervals are inconsistently chosen (the narrower MH interval is printed where the cluster interval is wide) and the primary p-values rely on a sandwich estimator with few effective clusters; and (iv) the screening conclusions (relative not absolute timing; "can help prioritise") are not supported and in places contradicted by Table 3 and the pooled AUCs. With the corrections in sections A and B the paper could be a useful, appropriately cautious RIPR article reporting a modest, heterogeneous, time-limited association that is not usable for judging individual papers.
