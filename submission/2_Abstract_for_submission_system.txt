Background: Authors and evaluators use a journal's recent time to acceptance to predict the wait for a new paper, but such forecasts are rarely scored against later outcomes, and the simplest is over-confident.

Methods: We used publisher-deposited received and accepted dates for 1,737,272 articles in 800 Springer Nature, Wiley and Taylor & Francis journals. Forecasts used only papers visible at each quarterly origin, and parameters learned from earlier data used only cohorts whose outcomes were complete at the origin. We propose CV0, which smooths a journal's last 12 months of durations by the cohort-to-cohort shift variance estimated from earlier cohorts, and compared it with empirical quantiles, width inflation, quantile recalibration and adaptive conformal updating over six origins (4,486 journal-origins). Hypotheses were prespecified; the duration cap was changed after the first run, so both caps are reported. The method, with parameters learned on the frame, was tested on 118 external PubMed journals.

Results: Journal history lowered forecast loss by 15.7% against pooled forecasts (two original origins). Under the strict protocol, empirical 80% intervals covered 1.9 points less than nominal, and the apparent shortfall depended on how slow papers were truncated (+2.2 to −2.4 points across caps of 270 days to none). CV0 moved coverage to +0.5 points and lowered mean absolute coverage error from 7.2 to 6.8 points (about 4.5 by chance), with lower pinball loss. It beat width inflation, recalibration and adaptive updating by about 0.3 points, but was better than F0 at 42% of journal-origins and worse at 38%, and over-covered Taylor & Francis journals. The pre-specified mechanism test was weak (r = 0.15) and failed at the 365-day cap. On external journals CV0 beat empirical quantiles at both caps but width inflation only at the 365-day cap. The 72-day calibration slope rose from 0.89 to 0.92.

Conclusions: A journal's recent history is a useful forecast whose intervals should be widened for cohort shift; learning the widening from earlier complete cohorts gives a small improvement that is not uniform across journals or publishers. Evaluations must handle truncation of slow papers. An open-source package and browser tool are provided.
